The Growth Edge · Speaking

Twenty-four questions, no right answers.

Pick a question, take a position, and defend it out loud. Some of these have a clean answer. Most don't, and that's exactly the point.

01

Comfort Zone vs. Growth Edge

On risk, readiness, and the discomfort that's actually doing something useful.

—
What separates a stretch assignment from a setup for failure?
Is the difference in the assignment itself, or in how much support comes with it?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"For me, the difference is resourcing: a stretch assignment usually comes with someone checking in on you. A setup for failure looks identical on paper, but nobody's actually invested in whether you succeed."
—
Is it possible to overprepare for a challenge?
What would that even look like in practice?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"You can overprepare in one specific way: when preparing becomes a way of avoiding starting. At some point, more research stops adding information and starts being a stall tactic."
—
What role does support, a mentor, a co-lead, play in turning a reckless risk into a calculated one?
Can the exact same decision be reckless for one person and calculated for another?
Need inspiration?
Real-world reference
"Whitney Johnson's S-Curve framework suggests real support usually means someone a few steps ahead of you on the same curve, not simply someone above you."
—
When should a company push someone past their comfort zone, and when is that simply unfair?
Who should get to decide where that line is, the company or the individual?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"It's fair when the person has a real say in the decision. It stops being fair the moment it's assigned without consent, disguised as an opportunity."
—
What is lost when someone always waits until they feel completely ready?
Is there anything gained by waiting, too?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"What's lost is momentum, and often the assignment itself, since someone else says yes in the meantime. What's gained is avoiding a genuinely bad fit, which does happen sometimes."
—
Can imposter syndrome ever be useful?
If so, what is it actually protecting a person from?
Need inspiration?
Real-world reference
"Basima Tewfik's research, published in Harvard Business Review, found people with strong imposter feelings were rated as more interpersonally effective by colleagues, the doubt made them more attentive, not less capable."
02

Why Feedback Culture Is Harder Than It Looks

On honesty, tact, and who actually carries the responsibility.

—
Why do most workplaces claim to value honesty but quietly reward sugarcoating?
Is that a failure of individuals, or of the system around them?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"It's systemic more than personal: performance reviews tie feedback to numbers that affect pay, so managers soften language to avoid conflict, even when they see the real problem clearly."
—
What responsibility does the person receiving feedback have, not just the person giving it?
Can feedback fail even when it's delivered perfectly?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"The receiver has to actually ask clarifying questions instead of absorbing feedback silently. Even excellent feedback dies on arrival without that."
—
Is written feedback, email, Slack, ever a real substitute for a conversation?
What does a written message lose that a conversation keeps?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"Written feedback loses tone and timing, you can't read the pause before someone responds, which is often where the real information lives."
—
What's the difference between feedback that changes behavior and feedback that just makes someone feel judged?
Can the exact same words do either, depending on delivery?
Need inspiration?
Real-world reference
"Kim Scott's Radical Candor framework argues it comes down to combining directness with visible care. Without both together, even accurate feedback tends to get rejected."
—
How does power change what "honest" should look like?
Should a boss giving feedback to a report sound different from a peer giving feedback to a peer?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"Yes: a boss's "honest" feedback carries consequences a peer's doesn't, so it usually needs more care, not less directness."
—
Should feedback culture apply upward, employees giving feedback to leaders?
What makes that version of the conversation harder?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"It's harder because the risk is asymmetric, the employee usually has more to lose than the leader does if the conversation goes badly."
03

The Ambition Trap: Contentment or Complacency?

On plateaus, restlessness, and how to tell the two apart.

—
Is there a point where ambition stops being productive and starts being self-destructive?
What would that turning point actually look like?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"The turning point is usually when the goal stops being about the work itself and becomes purely about comparison to other people."
—
What signals a career plateau before you consciously notice it?
Is it visible from the inside at all, or only in hindsight?
Need inspiration?
Real-world reference
"Whitney Johnson's S-Curve describes this as the flattening top of the curve: learning slows dramatically, but it often still feels like competence, not stagnation."
—
Can two people with identical careers, on paper, have completely different relationships to ambition?
What would explain the difference between them?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"The difference is usually whether the person chose the plateau, or just stopped noticing it."
—
Is contentment a skill, or just the absence of pressure?
Can it be learned, or does it only happen to you?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"It's a skill, because it requires actively deciding to stop measuring yourself against a moving target. That takes practice, not just luck."
—
What role does comparison to other people play in whether ambition feels healthy or exhausting?
Would ambition still exist at all without anyone to compare yourself to?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"Almost all of it, honestly. Remove other people from the picture and most "ambition" turns out to be about relative position, not the work itself."
—
If restlessness isn't tied to a specific goal, what is it actually about?
Is it always a problem to solve, or sometimes just a phase to sit with?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"Sometimes it isn't a problem to solve at all, just a signal that something unrelated to career actually needs attention."
04

Redefining Success on Your Own Terms

On inherited definitions, and what's left once you remove them.

—
How much of someone's definition of success is shaped by fear of judgment rather than genuine desire?
Is it possible to fully separate the two?
Need inspiration?
Real-world reference
"Psychologist Megan Dalla-Camina argues most inherited definitions of success are really about avoiding disappointment from someone specific, a parent, a mentor, more than genuine desire."
—
Is it possible to redefine success without changing your actual circumstances?
Or does the definition only really count once it changes a decision?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"It probably only counts once it changes an actual decision. Otherwise it's just a nicer story about the exact same life."
—
What would have to be true for someone to feel successful even without external recognition?
Is that realistic, or mostly theoretical?
Need inspiration?
Real-world reference
"A career-success survey from Florida Atlantic University found work-life balance and genuine interest in the work ranked above wealth for most people. Recognition wasn't even near the top."
—
Should a definition of success stay the same across an entire career, or change with it?
What would force it to change?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"It should probably change. A definition of success at 25 that never updates by 45 was likely borrowed, not built."
—
What's the risk of defining success too narrowly? Too broadly?
Which mistake is more common, and which is more costly?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"Too narrow, and one setback ruins everything. Too broad, and nothing ever counts as achieving it. Narrow is more common, and probably more costly."
—
If you removed every measure of success that other people can actually see, what would be left?
Would there be anything left at all?
Need inspiration?
Example answer
"Honestly, that's the real test. If nothing is left once you remove what others can see, the definition was never fully yours to begin with."
Before You Start Talking

Language for taking a position

You don't need to agree with your partner, and you don't need a final answer. Use these to disagree, concede a point, or shift the frame without losing the thread.

"I'd push back on that."
Eu discordaria disso.
"Fair point, but consider this..."
Ponto válido, mas considere isso...
"On the flip side..."
Por outro lado...
"Let's argue their strongest case first."
Vamos primeiro defender o argumento mais forte do outro lado.
"Playing devil's advocate here..."
Só pra provocar o contraponto...
"I see where you're coming from, but..."
Entendo seu ponto de vista, mas...
