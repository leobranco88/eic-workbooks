Starting Over

Some verbs and adjectives always travel with the same preposition. Marina is about to find that out, one goodbye at a time.

What are dependent prepositions?

Some verbs and adjectives are married to a specific preposition. It's not about meaning, it's about habit: native speakers always say worried ABOUT, never "worried on" or "worried for". You don't choose the preposition, the verb or adjective chooses it for you. And there's a second rule hiding inside the first one: whenever a verb comes right after one of these prepositions, it takes the -ING form. Interested in learning, not interested in learn.

Case 01 The Decision

Marina had been dreaming of a change for years, but she never imagined it would happen so fast. When the job offer from a design studio in Vancouver arrived, she was excited about the opportunity, but she was also terrified of leaving everything familiar behind. Her friends were surprised she was even considering it. "Are you sure you're capable of starting over in a new country?" her best friend asked. Marina laughed nervously. She wasn't sure of anything, but she was tired of waiting for the right moment. Some decisions, she realized, you just have to believe in.

Case 02 The Preparation

The weeks before the move were chaotic. Marina was responsible for organizing everything alone: the visa, the flights, the apartment. She spent hours researching, and she quickly got tired of filling out forms. Her mother worried about her going alone, and Marina, though she tried to sound confident, was secretly nervous about the interview she still had to do once she landed. Still, she was looking forward to exploring a new city, even if she was a little afraid of the cold. At night, she dreamed of walking through snow for the first time.

Case 03 The Goodbye

On her last night in São Paulo, Marina's apartment was full of boxes and half-empty suitcases. Her family gathered for dinner, and nobody talked much about the flight the next morning. Her father, who wasn't good at expressing emotion, simply said he was proud of her courage. Her sister complained about the silence in the apartment already, joking that she wasn't used to Marina being so quiet. Marina smiled, but she was thinking about all the small things she would miss: the smell of coffee, the noise of the street, Sunday lunches.

good AT proud OF complain ABOUT think ABOUT
Case 04 The Arrival

Vancouver welcomed Marina with grey skies and cold air, but also with something unexpected: kindness. Her new coworkers were interested in her story, and one of them insisted on showing her around the neighborhood on her first weekend. Marina relied on her broken English at first, but she was surprised by how quickly people adapted to her accent. She still depends on her savings while she waits for her first paycheck, and some nights she still dreams of home. But when she looks out at the mountains, she knows she made the right call. She is finally capable of building the life she always wanted.

Questions that end in a preposition

In Portuguese, the preposition usually moves to the front of the question. In English, it's the opposite: in everyday conversation, the preposition almost always stays at the end, right where it was attached to the verb or adjective. Moving it to the front isn't wrong, it's just extremely formal, the kind of English you'd read in a legal document, never the kind you'd hear at dinner.

Very formal (rare)About what are you worried?
Natural (always use this)What are you worried about?

Try It: Marina's Interview

1. She is excited about the opportunity.
2. She is responsible for the whole move.
3. Her coworkers are interested in her story.
4. She relies on her savings.
