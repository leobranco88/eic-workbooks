Section 01 · Grammar Reference

Causative Verbs

make · have · let · get — four verbs that describe who causes what to happen.

A causative verb shows that one person causes another person (or thing) to do something — or that one force causes an effect. In the world of Brave New World, causative verbs are everywhere: the state makes you comply, lets you believe you are free, has everything arranged for you, and gets you to want exactly what the system needs.

make Force / No choice
Make means to force or compel someone to do something. The person has no real choice. The cause is external and often unwanted.
Structure
make + person + bare infinitive
Examples in context
Soma makes you feel nothing. You do not choose to feel nothing — the drug produces the effect.
The World State makes citizens consume constantly — not through direct orders, but through conditioning.
What makes you want the things you want? Huxley's answer: the system does.
have Arrange / Delegate
Have means to arrange for someone to do something, or to cause something to be done. It implies delegation — often without the other person having a say.
Structure
have + person + bare infinitive
have + thing + past participle
Examples in context
The state has citizens conditioned before birth. The conditioning is arranged — the citizen is the object.
The algorithm has your desires designed for you before you know you have them.
Bernays had the public sold a manufactured identity without them noticing.
let Permit / Allow
Let means to allow or permit something to happen. The subject has the power to stop it — and chooses not to. In Huxley's world, this is the most insidious structure.
Structure
let + person + bare infinitive
Examples in context
The World State lets you believe you chose this life. Permission is the most elegant form of control.
The platform lets you curate your own feed — while the algorithm decides what you will see.
Consumer culture lets you feel like an individual while purchasing the same identity as millions of others.
get Persuade / Convince
Get means to persuade or convince someone to do something — or to manage to make something happen. Unlike make, there is effort and often some degree of agency involved.
Structure
get + person + to + infinitive
get + thing + past participle
Examples in context
Bernays got women to smoke by linking cigarettes to the concept of freedom.
The attention economy gets you to stay on the platform by making leaving feel like a loss.
How does a system get people to want their own servitude? That is Huxley's central question.
The key differences
Dimension make have let get
Core idea Force No choice given. Arrange Something is delegated. Permit Permission is granted. Persuade Effort to convince.
Infinitive form Bare infinitive
make someone do		 Bare infinitive or past participle
have someone do / have something done		 Bare infinitive
let someone do		 To + infinitive
get someone to do
Agency of person None. External force. Minimal. Arranged for them. They could act — they are allowed to. Some — they are persuaded, not forced.
In Huxley's world Soma makes you feel nothing. The state has citizens conditioned before birth. The system lets you believe you are free. Conditioning gets you to want your own cage.
A closer look
have / get something done

The agent disappears — but someone always does the work.

When you use have something done or get something done, the most important thing to understand is this: you are not doing it yourself. Someone else is doing it for you — or to you. The structure shifts focus onto the result and away from the person who actually performed the action.

have something done
You arrange for someone else to do something. The other person — a professional, a service, a system — is the real agent. They often go unmentioned entirely.
have + thing + past participle
Who actually does it?
I had my apartment cleaned.
→ A cleaner cleaned it. Not me.
Lyra has her feed curated every morning.
→ An algorithm curates it. Not Lyra.
Citizens have their desires designed for them.
→ The system designs them. The citizen is the object, not the subject of the action.
get something done
Similar to have something done, but implies more effort on your part — you had to make it happen. Often used in informal contexts or when the process required persistence.
get + thing + past participle
Who actually does it?
I finally got my phone fixed.
→ A technician fixed it. I made it happen.
She got her article published.
→ An editor published it. She pursued it.
How did Bernays get the idea sold to the public?
→ The media, the press, the culture sold it. Bernays engineered the process.
The key insight
Who appears as subject
The person who arranged it — not the person who did it.
Who disappears
The person who actually performed the action. They are grammatically erased.
In Huxley's world
Citizens have their identities designed, their desires curated, their happiness arranged — by a system that never appears in the sentence.
The most chilling sentence in Brave New World is a let sentence: "The World State lets you feel happy." Permission is the most invisible form of control — because it looks exactly like freedom.

Section 04 · Homework

Before the next class.

Three tasks. Write your answers and bring them to the lesson.

This should take around 20 minutes. Do not look up the answers — write what feels right first, then check the reference section if you are unsure.
Task 01 · Transform make · have · let · get

Rewrite each sentence using the causative verb in brackets. You may need to change the form.

Task 02 · Error Correction Find the mistake

Each sentence has one error. Find it, correct it, write the full correct sentence.

Task 03 · Free Writing

Write 4–6 sentences about your own life using causative verbs. Use at least three different causatives. Think about: what makes you do things you would rather not do? What does the system let you believe? What has been designed for you that you did not choose?
