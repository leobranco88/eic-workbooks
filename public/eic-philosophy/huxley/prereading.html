The Context

When and why it was written.

Huxley wrote this in four months in 1931. The world outside was not doing well.

The year
1931
Two years into the Great Depression. Mass unemployment across Europe and the US.
The threat Huxley saw
Not poverty. Comfort.
While others feared fascism and economic collapse, Huxley feared something quieter: a world where people would give up freedom willingly, in exchange for pleasure.
The comparison he made
Huxley vs. Orwell
Two visions of the future. Two entirely different mechanisms of control. And only one of them requires the people to suffer.
The comparison intellectuals keep returning to
George Orwell
Nineteen Eighty-Four · 1949
The mechanism of control
Fear. Pain. Surveillance. Force. The Party controls through terror — the Thought Police, Room 101, the telescreen, public executions. Citizens are watched constantly. Dissent is detected and punished. The past is rewritten. Language itself is reduced — Newspeak eliminates the words needed to think forbidden thoughts.
"If you want a picture of the future, imagine a boot stamping on a human face — forever."
O'Brien · Nineteen Eighty-Four · Part III
The result People want to resist. They are broken before they can. Winston Smith knows something is wrong. He just cannot survive the consequences of acting on it.
Aldous Huxley
Brave New World · 1932
The mechanism of control
Pleasure. Comfort. Distraction. Desire. The World State controls through gratification — soma, promiscuity, entertainment, and a social system in which everyone loves their predetermined role. No one is watching you. There is no need — because no one wants to do anything worth watching for.
"And if ever, by some unlucky chance, anything unpleasant should somehow happen, why, there's always soma to give you a holiday from the facts."
Aldous Huxley · Brave New World · Chapter 9
The result People do not want to resist. The question of freedom never arises — not because it is forbidden, but because it feels unnecessary. You cannot rebel against a life you love.
The bridge — Neil Postman, 1985
In his book Amusing Ourselves to Death, the media theorist Neil Postman argued that most intellectuals in the twentieth century were watching for the wrong dystopia. They were watching for Orwell — for censorship, state violence, and surveillance. What was actually arriving, Postman wrote, looked much more like Huxley.
"Orwell feared that the truth would be concealed from us. Huxley feared the truth would be drowned in a sea of irrelevance. Orwell feared we would become a captive culture. Huxley feared we would become a trivial culture."
Neil Postman · Amusing Ourselves to Death · Foreword, 1985
Postman was writing about television in 1985. The argument applies with considerably more force to the attention economy of 2024.

The reason intellectuals keep returning to this comparison: Orwell's world is horrifying and obvious. Huxley's world is seductive and invisible. And the mechanism that makes it invisible is the same mechanism that makes it enjoyable. That is why it is harder to resist.
"A really efficient totalitarian state would be one in which the all-powerful executive of political bosses and their army of managers control a population of slaves who do not have to be coerced, because they love their servitude."
Aldous Huxley · Brave New World (Foreword, 1946)
The Author

Who was Aldous Huxley?

A man who saw the twentieth century arriving before most people noticed.

Aldous Leonard Huxley
Huxley
1894 – 1963 · British novelist, philosopher, essayist
Huxley came from one of England's most distinguished intellectual families. His grandfather was the biologist Thomas Henry Huxley — ""Darwin's Bulldog"." His brother Julian was one of the most influential evolutionary biologists of the century.

Aldous himself lost most of his sight as a teenager, which forced him to develop an exceptional capacity for listening, thinking, and synthesising ideas across disciplines. He was not a scientist — but he read everything scientists produced, and translated it into literary form.

By 1931, he had already written several successful novels and was deeply worried about the direction of modern civilisation: mass production, behaviourism, consumer culture, and the increasing power of technology to shape human experience without people noticing.
In 1958, Huxley wrote Brave New World Revisited — a non-fiction follow-up in which he argued that the dystopia he had imagined as fiction in 1932 was arriving faster than he had predicted. He was not triumphant about being right. He was alarmed.
The Book

What happens in Brave New World.

You do not need to have read it. But you need to know where it takes place.

The novel · 1932
Brave New World
Published 1932 · Set in the year 2540 AF ("After Ford")
The story takes place in a World State where human beings are no longer born — they are manufactured. Citizens are conditioned from birth to love their place in society, to consume constantly, and to dissolve any discomfort with a drug called soma.

There is no war. No poverty. No suffering. There is also no art, no genuine love, no family, no God, and no memory of what life used to mean. Everyone is happy. No one is free.

The protagonist, Bernard Marx, begins to feel that something is missing — but cannot name what it is. When he meets John, a man raised outside the World State, the collision between two ways of being human becomes the heart of the novel.
The Ideas

Three ideas that will anchor our class.

Not a summary. A map of what we will be discussing.

01
The central argument
The perfect cage has no bars.
Huxley's most disturbing insight is not that people can be controlled by force. It is that they can be controlled by pleasure. A population that is happy, entertained, and comfortable will not resist — because they do not feel anything to resist against. The bars are invisible because they are made of desire, not iron.
02
The mechanism
Manufactured desire is still desire.
In the World State, citizens are conditioned to want exactly what the system needs them to want. The desire feels genuine from the inside — it is experienced as preference, not as manipulation. This is the mechanism that makes the system stable. You cannot resist a desire you believe is freely chosen.
03
The question for us
How much of this is already here?
Huxley was writing about the future. But in our class, we will ask whether the mechanisms he described — behaviourist conditioning, manufactured desire, the numbing effect of constant entertainment — are science fiction or a description of the present.
"Actual happiness always looks pretty squalid in comparison with the overcompensations for misery. And, of course, stability isn't nearly so spectacular as instability."
Aldous Huxley · Brave New World
Key Vocabulary

Words you will need.

Click each card to expand. These will appear in class — arrive already familiar.

manufactured desire
A want that was deliberately created in you by an external agent — before you had any spontaneous reason to want it. The desire feels authentic because it was personalised. That is what makes it effective.
desejo fabricado / necessidade criada artificialmente
"Lyra did not wake up wanting the jacket. The algorithm identified the gap before she did, and filled her feed with it. By 9am, the desire was hers."
conditioning
The process of shaping behaviour, preferences, and identity through repeated exposure — so that certain responses become automatic and feel natural. In the World State, conditioning begins before birth.
condicionamento / modelagem do comportamento por exposicao repetida
"In Brave New World, children are conditioned to love the outdoors but hate flowers — because loving flowers would not drive consumption."
the World State
The name of the global government in Brave New World. Its motto is "Community, Identity, Stability." Stability is the highest value — not freedom, not truth, not love.
o Estado Mundial / governo global em Admiravel Mundo Novo
"The World State does not need police or prisons. It needs soma, entertainment, and an education system that makes the question of freedom feel irrelevant."
soma
The happiness drug in Brave New World. It dissolves discomfort, anxiety, and existential questioning — with no side effects and no hangover. It is distributed by the state and taken voluntarily.
soma / droga da felicidade em Admiravel Mundo Novo
"Soma does not make you happy. It makes unhappiness feel unnecessary. That is a more efficient solution."
behaviourism
A school of psychology that argues human behaviour can be predicted, shaped, and controlled through external stimuli — rewards and punishments — without needing to understand inner states. Huxley found this terrifying in its implications for social control.
behaviorismo / psicologia do comportamento por estimulos externos
"The algorithm does not need to understand you. It just needs to know which stimuli produce which responses. That is behaviourism at scale."
stability vs. freedom
The central tension of the novel. The World State has chosen stability — the elimination of conflict, suffering, and uncertainty. But freedom, Huxley argues, requires the possibility of all three. You cannot have genuine choice without the risk of choosing badly.
estabilidade vs. liberdade / o dilema central do romance
"A comfortable life with no real decisions is stable. It is not free. Huxley's question is whether most people, given the choice, would notice the difference."
The most important distinction for our class: comfort is not the same as freedom. Huxley's argument is that the most effective systems of control are the ones that make you forget this.
