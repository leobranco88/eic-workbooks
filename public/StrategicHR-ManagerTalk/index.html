What does a manager
actually do?

Employees at your clients keep saying the same things: my manager checks everything, doesn’t understand our work, promises dates the team can’t meet. A sketch starts the conversation, and you learn to hear the pattern before you take a side.

Meet Daniel. He leads a team of six at a client company, and his analysts have started to ask for transfers. Describe the pattern, then ask what worries the manager. Everything is also on the Cheat sheet, ready to print.

4 Boss?

Could you say it about the manager?

People are talking about the manager of a client team. Read each sentence and decide: does it work in a meeting with the client, or does it belong with friends?

The test is not whether the client is relaxed. Ask yourself: could this sentence go into a written note about the manager and still be fair? If yes, thumb up. If it judges him, or only works as a joke or an image, thumb down.

Example
Three people told me they don’t know what he expects of them.
Up. It reports what people said. You could write it in a file.
Honestly, he’s a control freak.
Down. A label, and a joke. In a note, it would sound unfair.

5 The ask

Four moves, in this order.

You: HR

You are an HR consultant. An analyst at a client company stops you after a workshop and says:

Employee
“My manager micromanages everything.”

Don’t conclude anything yet. Moves 1 and 2 are with the employee, moves 3 and 4 are with the manager. You will meet the manager again in part 8.

The marked part is the chunk. Keep it, and change the rest to fit your case.

1
Ask what it looks like
The employee gives you a complaint. Ask for a scene: what the manager does, when and how often.
Can you walk me through a typical day? What does your manager actually do?
2
Say it back
Repeat what you heard with no adjectives, and check it.
Let me make sure I understand: he asks for an update on every task, every day.
3
Find the worry
With the manager: ask what he wants to be sure of, not why he controls.
What are you trying to be sure of? What would you need to see to feel confident?
4
Agree on a rhythm
End with a check-in rhythm, an owner and a date.
Could we agree on a check-in rhythm? You get visibility, and the team gets room to work.

6 The answer

What comes back.

You: HR

When the manager pushes back.

You are speaking to the manager. He defends himself with results or with experience. Don’t argue: ask what he wants to be sure of.
“I’m not micromanaging. I’m being thorough.”
I understand. What are you trying to be sure of? Let’s find a way to give you that.
“I have the experience. I know what’s best.”
That experience is valuable. Which parts of the team’s work would you like to see up close?
“If I don’t check, things slip.”
What has slipped before? Let’s set a few checkpoints on the risky parts.
You: HR

When the director wants the date.

You are speaking to the director. The promise is already made. Don’t say the team is slow: bring the estimate and two options.
“The director wants the date.”
I recommend we confirm the estimate with the team before we commit.
“We can’t say no to the client.”
We don’t have to say no. We can offer a later date, or a smaller scope.
“The team is just slow.”
Before we call it slow, let’s compare the estimate with the workload.
7 The tool

Match the style to the task.

Situational Leadership II, by Ken Blanchard, says the right style depends on the person on that task: their skill and their confidence. The same person can need different styles on different tasks.

StyleIn the meeting · the example
Directing
New to the task and needs direction.
Here is how we do it. Let’s go through the first one together.
Coaching
Learning, but losing confidence.
What is hard so far? Let’s look at it together.
Supporting
Capable, but still cautious.
What do you think is the best approach? I’m here if you need me.
Delegating
Experienced and confident.
This is yours. Update me at the milestones.

Our reading. Micromanaging is using directing with someone who needs delegating. A good question for the client: For this task, how much direction does this person need?

Two views on control.

The manager in the sketch says he needs to know. Here is how that argument can sound in a real meeting. Who do you agree with, and why?

Manager

HR consultant
That’s a fair worry. What has slipped before?
Manager
Two reports went to the director with errors last quarter.
Pause

The manager just gave you a reason. You are the HR consultant. What do you say next? Say it aloud in two sentences, then read on.

HR consultant
Then your checking has a reason. What would you need to see to feel safe without asking every day?
Manager
A way to see progress. Right now I can only find out by asking.
HR consultant
That is useful. The problem may be visibility, not trust. Could the team update the tracker twice a week, so you don’t have to ask?

8 The case

The new team leader.

An original case. All people and facts are fictional.

You: HR

Daniel leads a team of six analysts at a client company. Last year he moved from sales to operations. The analysts say he asks for updates three times a day, rewrites their reports and does not know how the reporting system works. Last month he promised the director a monthly close in three days. The team’s estimate was five. When an analyst said the date was unrealistic, Daniel answered, “I need to know what’s taking so long.” Two analysts have asked for a transfer.

Your turn

You are the HR consultant. Talk to Daniel first. Ask three questions. Then give two recommendations, and agree on one thing to check in four weeks.

  • The main issue may be…
  • First, I need to know…
  • I recommend… because…
  • Daniel will…
  • In four weeks, we will check…
Answer only when asked: (1) The director announced the three-day close at a board meeting, and Daniel did not feel he could change it. (2) Two reports went to the director with errors last quarter, and Daniel was blamed. (3) He asks for updates because the tracker is rarely updated, so he cannot see progress. (4) He has never used the reporting system. He tried to learn, but felt embarrassed to ask his team. (5) Daniel says the team does not tell him about problems early. (6) One analyst missed a deadline in September and did not warn anyone. If needed, push back with: “If I don’t check, things slip.”

The main issue may be visibility, not trust. First, I need to know what Daniel is trying to be sure of. I recommend two checkpoints a week instead of daily updates, and a tracker that the team keeps current. I also recommend that Daniel shadow the team for a day to learn the reporting system. The September deadline still needs a conversation, starting with a specific example. Let’s check in again in four weeks to look at how often he asks for updates, and whether the team warns him early.
