Strategic HR · Performance talk

Ask what
they did.

A performance talk runs on examples and soft questions. Nobody helps anyone by saying She's difficult.

Meet Luisa. She is an analyst, and her manager calls her “negative”. Before you label someone, ask what they did. Then look at the workload. Four short clips start the conversation, and this page follows Luisa to the end. Everything is also on the Cheat sheet, ready to print.

1 The clips

Four short clips, four opinions.

Before you watch

Talk about these first. Then watch and see if the speakers agree with you.

  1. Think of a manager who made someone better at their job. What did that manager actually do?
    Start with

    She gave him… / He trusted me with…

    Ideas

    She gave him a stretch assignment and told him why she chose him.

    He gave honest feedback every week, and he always started with what worked.

  2. Which labels do people hear at work? Who gives them, and who decides they are true?
    Start with

    Labels are usually given by… and rarely…

    Ideas

    “Difficult”, “negative”, “not a team player”. A manager usually gives them, and often nobody checks.

    A label sticks because people repeat it, not because someone showed evidence.

  3. If a manager calls an employee “negative”, what should HR do first?
    Start with

    First, I would ask…

    Ideas

    First, I would ask for a specific example: what did the person say or do, and when?

    I would listen first and check the facts. I wouldn't agree or disagree yet.

What question does the speaker want leaders to ask when someone is labeled?

“What did they actually do?”

Well, you show me someone that’s been labeled where they work, and I guarantee you they’re one of your best people.

See, in most organizations, people wearing the worst labels, they’re the best people in the building.

Let me explain.

I spent decades building high-performing teams, and over those years I’ve learned to read labels differently.

They’re labeled difficult, negative, not a team player.

Understand this: labels are rarely about performance. They get handed out when someone says a truth that somebody above them did not want to say out loud.

Think about who actually earns that label.

The hardest workers are deepest in the work, right? So they see what is broken first. And because they care, they actually say something.

That is the crime. They saw it, and they said it.

Now, what about who never gets labeled?

The one who coasts. The one who nods in every meeting and never opens her mouth, never raises a problem.

You cannot be labeled if you never say anything.

Silence is how mediocrity stays safe.

And for anyone in a leadership seat, here’s the move.

The next time a label gets handed to you, you ask a question: “What did they actually do?”

Not how they made someone feel, because that’s nonsense.

What did they do?

Then talk

Can someone have a good point and still say it badly? Where does HR draw the line?

Start with

The point may be right, but…

Ideas

Yes. The content can be right and the tone can still hurt the team. HR can look at content, behavior and impact separately.

That's why we ask for a specific example before we decide.

2 Boss?

Could you say it to your boss?

People are talking about Luisa. Read each sentence and decide: does it work in a meeting with your boss, or does it belong with friends?

The test is not whether your boss is relaxed. Ask yourself: could this sentence go into a written note about Luisa and still be fair? If yes, thumb up. If it judges her, or only works as a joke or an image, thumb down.

Example
She raised a problem about the deadline and showed her numbers.
Up. It says what she did. You could write it in her file.
Honestly, I think her whole project will fall apart.
Down. A dramatic prediction, not a fact. In a note, it would sound unfair.

3 The ask

Four moves, in this order.

You: HR

You are an HR business partner. After a meeting, a manager stops you and says this about Luisa, the analyst:

Manager
“Luisa is negative. I don't want her on the new project.”

Don't agree yet, and don't argue. These four moves turn a label into a conversation about facts. You will come back to Luisa in part 6.

The marked part is the chunk. Keep it, and change the rest to fit your case.

1
Ask for an example
The manager gives you a label. Ask for the fact behind it.
Can you give me a specific example? What did she actually do?
2
Describe, don't label
The manager tells you what happened. Say it back with no adjectives.
Let's stick to what happened: she said the deadline was unrealistic and shared her numbers.
3
Check the workload
Before you decide it is an attitude problem, look at the load.
Before we call it a performance issue, let's look at her workload.
4
Agree on a check-in
End the meeting with an action, an owner and a date.
I recommend we share the extra tasks. Let's check in again in four weeks.

Same manager, same meeting. The left side is what many people say first. The right side is what works.

Too blunt → Say this
She's difficult.→She challenged the new deadline in Monday's meeting.
He's not a team player.→I'd like to understand the collaboration concern. Can you give me an example?
She's burned out.→She's been working late. Let's ask her how she's doing.
You give her too much work.→Her workload has grown. Which task can we move?
4 The answer

What comes back.

You: HR

When the manager pushes back.

The reply is usually about the deadline or the budget. Ask what can move or be shared.
“We still need to meet the deadline.”
I understand. Which task can we delay, or share?
“She's my best person. She can handle it.”
That's why we should protect her time. Who can take part of it?
“We don't have the budget to hire anyone.”
Understood. Could we move one deliverable by a week?
You: employee

When you are the one doing too much.

Say yes to the project, then negotiate what goes.
Say yes, then ask
I'd be glad to join the project. Can we review my current priorities first?
Negotiate
I can do this, but which task should I delay?
Set a limit
I can't take that on this week. I can start on Monday.

5 The tool

Situation, behavior, impact.

HR doesn't ask who the problem is. It asks what is happening. This is a simple way to talk about a person without a label. The example: a manager called an analyst negative.

StepIn the meeting · the example
Situation
When and where it happened.
In Monday's team meeting.
Behavior
What you saw and heard. No adjectives.
You said the new deadline was unrealistic and showed your numbers.
Impact
The effect on you, the team or the work.
The team started to talk about priorities.
Intent
Optional. Ask what the person wanted to happen.
What did you want to happen?

Two views on impact.

The speaker in clip D says feelings are not the point. Here is how that argument can sound in a real meeting. Who do you agree with, and why?

Manager
Feelings are part of the job. If she makes people uncomfortable, that's a performance problem.
HR partner
It can be. Impact is part of the story. But “uncomfortable” is not evidence yet, so let's start with what she did.
Manager
She said the deadline was impossible, in front of everyone.
Pause

The manager just gave you a fact. You are the HR partner. What do you say next? Say it aloud in two sentences, then read on.

HR partner
Then the observable behavior is: she raised a concern about the deadline and showed numbers. The impact is that the team now worries about it. That is useful information.
Manager
Or she's just negative.
HR partner
Maybe. What would we need to see to know?

6 The case

The reliable employee.

You: HR

An original case. All people and facts are fictional.

Luisa is an analyst in a team of eight people. Her work is accurate and she usually meets deadlines. Her manager gives her extra tasks because she is reliable. Last month she worked late three times a week. In a team meeting, she said a new deadline was unrealistic. Her manager now calls her “negative”. A new project is starting, but he does not want to include her.

Your turn

You are the HR business partner. Ask the manager three questions. Then give two recommendations, and agree on one thing to check in four weeks.

  • The main issue may be…
  • First, I need to know…
  • We should… because…
  • The manager will…
  • In four weeks, we will check…
Answer only when asked: (1) Luisa showed numbers that the new deadline is not possible. (2) Nobody was insulted in the meeting. (3) The manager gave her three extra tasks and took none away. (4) Two colleagues also worked late. (5) The project can be split between two people. (6) Two months ago Luisa answered a colleague sharply in a chat, and the colleague complained. Nobody spoke to her about it. If needed, push back with: “We still need to meet the deadline.”

We need to understand the workload before we call Luisa negative. She gave information about the deadline. I recommend a meeting to review priorities and share the extra tasks. We should also use clear criteria for the new project. And the sharp message to her colleague still needs a conversation, starting with a specific example. Let's check in again in four weeks to look at late working hours and whether the team meets its deadlines.
7 Before you go

Five questions before you label.

Ask these before anyone is called difficult. HR describes signs, never a diagnosis.

  1. What exactly did she say or do?
  2. When and where did it happen?
  3. What was the impact on the team?
  4. What was her workload at the time?
  5. What did she want to happen?
