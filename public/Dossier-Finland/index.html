Finland: trust or control?

Finland trains its teachers for years and does not inspect its schools. Then its results began to fall. This dossier gives you the facts, four voices and a debate.

Document A · Finland

The Model

By EIC Magazine · 1 min read · Select a passage to mark it. Hover over the dotted words to see them in Portuguese

For decades, Finland was the country that other governments visited when they wanted to understand good schools. A delegation of teachers and politicians traveled to Helsinki to find out what was different.

The first thing they noticed was who stood in front of the class. To teach in a Finnish basic or secondary school, you need a master’s degree. A subject teacher also needs at least 60 credits of pedagogical study: training in how to teach, not only in what to teach. Since the 1970s, this training has taken place in universities.

The second thing was what was missing. Finland has no system of school inspections. Nobody from outside visits classrooms to check that teachers are doing their job. Central control of textbooks ended in 1992, so teachers have wide freedom to choose their methods and materials. Even head teachers traditionally do not visit lessons to evaluate individual teachers.

Supporters of the model say the logic is simple: if you select and train teachers carefully, you do not need to watch them constantly. Parents trust the schools, so there is no need for control just for control’s sake. According to a 2014 World Economic Forum article, Finland did not even spend an especially large share of its budget on education compared with other OECD countries.

In this reading, trust came first, built through years of teacher education. Only then could control be reduced.

Document B · Finland

The Crack

By EIC Magazine · 1 min read · Select a passage to mark it. Hover over the dotted words to see them in Portuguese

Finland’s fame began with a test. PISA, run by the OECD, measures what 15-year-olds can do in reading, math and science. Finland took part for the first time in 2000, and its early results made the country a model for the world.

The latest round tells a more complicated story. In 2022, Finnish students scored 484 points in math, against an OECD average of 472. In reading they scored 490, against 476. Both results are still above average. But in reading the score fell by 30 points compared with 2018, and the fall was steeper than the OECD average. Math has been going down since 2006. There are now more weak performers in math and fewer top ones, and the gap between schools has grown somewhat.

Finland was not alone: scores fell in most countries in 2022, a round that took place after the pandemic. But Finland’s decline began long before that, which is why the debate matters.

Nobody agrees on the cause. Some critics blame pupil-led methods, which give students more control and less structure. Others point to digital tools in the classroom, and a 2018 study linked them to lower results in math and science. Defenders of the model answer that no single cause has been proved, and that the data do not show that trust itself failed.

Document C · Finland

The Response

By EIC Magazine · 1 min read · Select a passage to mark it. Hover over the dotted words to see them in Portuguese

In August 2025, a new rule came into force in Finnish schools. Under an amendment to the Basic Education Act, which Parliament passed in April, pupils aged seven to 16 may not use mobile phones during lessons. The exceptions are narrow: a teacher can allow a phone for learning, as a study aid, or for health reasons. If a phone disrupts teaching, a teacher or principal can confiscate it.

The law sets a minimum, and schools decide the rest. At one school in Varkaus, about 700 pupils keep their phones in their bags or in lockers all day, even at recess. In Tampere, pupils may use phones during breaks, but only outside the building. The National Agency for Education wants stricter rules than the law requires, including no phones at mealtimes.

The government explained the reform by pointing to results. The education minister said that reading and math skills had deteriorated, and that Finland wanted to create more space for learning and teaching. He added that schools were not leaving the digital world.

Reactions were mixed. One principal said the change had exceeded expectations. Some pupils agreed that phones are a distraction. Others said the law showed a lack of trust in them, a striking idea in a country whose school system was built on trust.

It is too early to know whether the law will improve results. For now, it raises an old question in a new form: when should a school trust, and when should it set limits?

13Take a position.

Your turn
Is a phone rule in school a sign of distrust or a way to support learning? Write 3 sentences.

Distrust: I think it can be a sign of distrust, because the law treats all pupils as if they cannot control themselves. Some students use their phones to study, so a general rule punishes them too.

Support: I see it as support for learning. Adults set limits on many things for children’s benefit, and a phone in the pocket is a strong distraction. A clear rule also protects pupils who find it hard to say no.

14Connect the documents.

Your turn
How does this law fit, or not fit, the idea that Finland’s system is built on trust? Write 2 to 3 sentences.

It partly fits, because the law still lets teachers and schools decide many details. But it also shows a limit to trust: when results fall, the state can step in with a national rule.

Document D · Voices

4 people · invented for this lesson

These four people are invented. Their words are not real quotes. Each one stands for a position that is common in the debate.

MK
Marja Koskinen · 47, math teacher in Tampere

I have taught for twenty years, and nobody has ever sat at the back of my classroom to grade me. People hear that and think we are left alone. We are not. We plan together, and we talk about every pupil who is struggling. Inspectors would bring paperwork, not insight. The phone rule? I use it, and pupils concentrate better. But I would never want the state to tell me how to teach fractions.

TL
Tuomas Lehtonen · 55, education researcher in Helsinki

I admire what Finland built, but admiration is not evidence. Reading scores have been falling for years, and a system that cannot explain why has a problem. Trust is fine when outcomes are good. When they fall, you need independent data, from outside the school, to see where help is needed. Calling that ‘control’ is a convenient way to avoid the question.

AV
Aino Virtanen · 16, high school student

Honestly, the phone rule annoys me. I use my phone to check words and my timetable, and now I have to ask permission. It feels like adults think we cannot be responsible. But I admit I pay more attention in class now, and at lunch my friends talk to each other instead of looking at screens. So I am confused.

JN
Jari Nieminen · 43, father of two students, Espoo

The phone law is the first thing in years that made me feel the system was listening to parents. What worries me is something else: schools in our city are very different, and I have no easy way to compare them. I want to trust my children’s teachers, but I would also like clear information about how the school is doing.

19Debate preparation.

Your turn
Choose a side: “A school system can rely on trust alone.” Write two reasons and one answer to the opposite side. Use at least one of these chunks: I see your point, but… · That depends on… · What worries me is…

For: Teachers who are well trained know their pupils better than an inspector who visits for a day. I see your point about results, but falling scores happened in many countries, so we cannot blame trust alone.

EThe Debate

Trust or control?

The motion

A school system can rely on trust more than on control.

One group defends

Trust

Schools work best when teachers are trusted to decide how to teach.

The other group defends

Control

Schools need outside checks and data, such as inspections and tests.

How the debate works

  1. 1Sides

    One group defends Trust. The other defends Control. The sides are drawn by lot, so nobody defends only what they already think, and the debate is fair.

    2 min

  2. 2Preparation

    Each group chooses 3 facts from the Evidence Bank and writes a claim.

    8 min

  3. 3Round 1

    Each side presents its main argument. No interruptions.

    2 min per side

  4. 4Round 2

    Each side answers the other side’s argument. This is the rebuttal.

    2 min per side

  5. 5Closing

    Each side sums up in 3 sentences.

    1 min per side

  6. 6Verdict

    The observers give their verdict: which side was more convincing, and why?

    3 min

One-to-one class: the teacher takes the side opposite to the student’s. The Evidence Bank is the same.
