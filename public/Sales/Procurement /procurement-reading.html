Article

From First Contact to Signed Deal

Adapted for classroom use · B1+ level · ~520 words

Every international purchase begins the same way: someone in a company decides they need something they cannot produce themselves. It could be a raw material, a component, a piece of equipment, or a service. Whatever it is, the next step is always finding a supplier — and that process, called procurement, is where the language of business becomes critical.

The first formal step is the Request for Quotation, or RFQ. This document is more than a price inquiry — it defines the product specifications, the quantity required, the expected lead time, the delivery location, and the preferred payment terms. A well-written RFQ saves weeks of repeated emails and clarifications. A poorly written one invites confusion, misquotations, and delays that can affect an entire production schedule.

When suppliers respond, they send a quotation. This is not yet a commitment — it is an offer. The purchasing team then compares quotations across multiple suppliers, evaluating not just price but also MOQ, reliability, warranty conditions, and the vendor's track record.

"We sent the same RFQ to five suppliers. Three never responded. One gave us a price with no lead time. Only one sent a complete quotation. That told us everything we needed to know about who to work with."

Purchasing manager, automotive sector, São Paulo

Once a supplier is selected, the buyer issues a Purchase Order (PO). This is a legally binding document. It locks in the unit price, the quantity, the delivery date, and the payment terms. Any changes after the PO is issued require a formal amendment — a process that takes time and creates risk on both sides.

For larger or longer-term relationships, a purchase agreement replaces or supplements the individual PO. This document goes deeper: it includes penalty clauses for late delivery, force majeure provisions, quality standards, and sometimes Incoterms that define exactly where responsibility transfers from the seller to the buyer.

None of this happens smoothly without clear communication. Brazilian purchasing professionals who work with international suppliers face a double challenge: they must understand commercial English well enough to catch a problematic clause in a contract, and confident enough to negotiate changes without damaging the relationship. That combination — reading precisely and speaking professionally — is what separates good procurement from great procurement.

procurement

pedido de cotação formal

lead time

The total time between placing an order and receiving the goods.

condições de pagamento
The agreed rules about when and how a buyer will pay the seller — for example, 30 days after the invoice date.
quotation

A formal document from a supplier stating the price, quantity, and conditions under which they will sell their goods.
MOQ (Minimum Order Quantity)
quantidade mínima de pedido
The smallest quantity a supplier is willing to sell in a single order.
Purchase Order (PO)
ordem de compra
A formal document issued by a buyer to a supplier, confirming the purchase of specific goods at an agreed price and quantity.
amendment
aditivo / alteração de contrato
A formal written change made to an existing contract or purchase order after it has been signed.
purchase agreement
contrato de fornecimento
A formal legal document that defines the terms of an ongoing supply relationship between a buyer and a supplier.
penalty clause
penalidade contratual
A financial consequence applied when one party fails to meet the contract terms — such as late delivery or quality failure.
force majeure
força maior
A contract provision that frees both parties from obligations when extraordinary events — such as disasters or conflicts — make fulfilment impossible.
Incoterm
incoterm
Internationally recognised trade terms that define where responsibility transfers from seller to buyer, and who pays for shipping and insurance.
clause
cláusula
A specific condition or requirement written into a contract or agreement.

Discussion

Connect the article to your own work. Tap "Model answer" to see an example response.

01

The article says a poorly written RFQ invites confusion and delays. Have you ever sent or received an unclear request? What went wrong?

Think about missing information — a quantity not specified, a delivery date not mentioned, or a product description that was too vague.

Model answer
"Yes, we once sent an RFQ without specifying whether the price should include freight or not. Three suppliers quoted FOB Shanghai and two quoted CIF Santos. When we compared them, the numbers looked completely different — but they were not comparable. We lost two weeks going back and forth to clarify. Now we always specify the Incoterm in the RFQ."
02

The purchasing manager in the article said that how a supplier responds to an RFQ "tells you everything." Do you agree? What does a supplier's response reveal about them?

Think about speed, completeness, tone, and attention to detail in the response.

Model answer
"I completely agree. A supplier who replies in 48 hours with a complete, well-structured quotation is showing us that they are organised and take us seriously. A supplier who takes two weeks and sends a vague price without lead time or MOQ is probably going to create the same problems later. The RFQ response is like a first interview — it gives you a preview of what the relationship will be like."
03

Have you ever had to negotiate a contract clause or a payment term in English? What was the hardest part — the vocabulary, the tone, or something else?

Think about a specific moment where you felt the language was a barrier — a call, an email, or a document you had to sign.

Model answer
"The hardest part for me was tone. I knew what I wanted to say — I wanted to push back on a 50% advance payment requirement. But I did not know how to say it without sounding aggressive or without closing the door on the negotiation. In Portuguese I would say something like 'isso não é viável para nós' — but directly translated that sounds too blunt in English. I ended up writing 'we would like to explore alternative payment arrangements' and it worked. But it took me 20 minutes to write one sentence."
04

The article mentions force majeure as something procurement teams now need to understand. Did COVID-19 or any other disruption force you to use — or wish you had understood — this kind of contract clause?

Think about 2020 and 2021 — supplier delays, cancelled orders, or agreements that had to be renegotiated.

Model answer
"In 2021, a supplier in China invoked force majeure to cancel a delivery we had already paid for in advance. I had never heard the term before and I had to look it up while on the call with them. They were right — they could not produce due to factory closures — but I felt completely unprepared. I did not know what questions to ask, what our rights were, or how to even respond professionally. That experience made me understand that contract language is not optional — it is part of the job."
