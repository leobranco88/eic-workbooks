The Clock Is Running — interpreting the SLA

Every case Renata handles has an invisible clock attached to it. A production line down is not the same as a request for paperwork — and knowing the difference, in minutes and hours, is exactly what the SLA is for.

Renata Lima · Customer Success, Altiva Biosolutions
01What Is an SLA, and When Is It Used?

Antes de entrar nos números da Altiva, vale entender o conceito em si.

Listen to this explanation
0:37 · What Is an SLA?

An SLA (Service Level Agreement) is a formal commitment about how fast and how well a service will be delivered — not just a promise to "do a good job," but a specific, measurable target with a real deadline attached to it.

It's used anywhere a client depends on a company to act within a bounded amount of time: IT support, logistics, contracted maintenance, and B2B supply relationships like Altiva's, where a delayed response can directly stop a client's production line. Without one, "soon" means something different to everyone. With one, everyone is working from the same clock.

✗ Without an SLA
"We'll get to it soon."
"It shouldn't take too long."
"Someone will look into it."
No way to say whether the response was actually fast enough.
✓ With an SLA
"We respond within 1 hour for critical issues."
"Resolution within 1 business day."
"This case is P2 — here's the target."
A clear, measurable standard both sides agreed to in advance.
Why it matters for Renata: an SLA isn't just a rule she has to follow — it's also what protects her. Instead of guessing case by case whether she's moving fast enough, she has a standard she can point to, explain, and be judged against fairly.

Internal Policy · Service Level Agreement (SLA)
Section 3: Service Level Agreement

3.1 — Priority Levels

Every case is assigned a priority level from P1 to P4 based on business impact. P1 — Critical covers issues like a production line stopped by a supply problem. P4 — Low covers things like a documentation request with no operational urgency.

3.2 — Response Time

Response time is measured from the moment the client's message arrives to the moment someone acknowledges it — not to the moment it's solved. A breach occurs the instant that window closes with no acknowledgment sent.

3.3 — Resolution Time

Resolution time is the total time until the case is fully closed, not just acknowledged. Response and resolution are tracked separately — meeting one does not guarantee meeting the other.

3.4 — Exclusions

The SLA clock pauses for delays caused by the client, and does not apply to issues involving the client's own equipment or process rather than Altiva's product. These are explicitly excluded from SLA calculations.

03Past Modals of Criticism

Quando um SLA é avaliado depois do fato, a gente usa "should have" e "shouldn't have" pra criticar o que aconteceu (ou não aconteceu).

What HappenedThe Critique
A P1 ticket sat unanswered for 3 hours. The team should have responded within 1 hour.
Renata promised a resolution time without checking the SLA first. She shouldn't have promised a timeline she hadn't confirmed.
A P4 documentation request was answered in 2 days, well inside the 5-day target. Nothing to criticize here — this one was handled exactly right.
Diego found out about a breached SLA from the client, not from Renata. Renata should have flagged the delay before the client noticed.
Renata treated a routine P3 request with P1-level urgency, pulling in Diego unnecessarily. She shouldn't have escalated it — a P3 doesn't call for that.
The pattern: should have + past participle criticizes something that didn't happen but needed to. shouldn't have + past participle criticizes something that did happen but shouldn't have. Both look back at a decision — they don't change what already happened.

05Case Review: Was the SLA Met?

Cinco casos reais. Identifique a prioridade, confira o prazo, e decida se o SLA foi cumprido.

Case 1 · P1
A client's production line stops because of a suspected contamination issue. They call at 10:00. Renata responds at 10:40, confirming the case is open and a technician is reviewing it.
SLA met. Under 3.1 and 3.2, P1 response time is 1 hour. A 40-minute response comfortably meets that target.
How Renata reflects
"We responded well within the window on this one — exactly how a P1 should have been handled."
Case 2 · P2
A client reports a shipment delay that could affect production in a few days — high priority, but not an emergency. They message at 9:00 Monday. Renata replies at 15:00 the same day.
SLA breached. P2 response time is 4 hours. Six hours passed before Renata replied — the team should have responded by 13:00.
How Renata reflects
"I should have caught this one sooner — a P2 shouldn't have waited six hours for a first reply."
Case 3 · P4
A client requests a copy of a Certificate of Analysis for their records — no urgency attached. They ask on Friday afternoon. Renata sends it the following Tuesday.
SLA breached. P4 response time is 1 business day. The next business day after Friday is Monday — Renata shouldn't have let it slip to Tuesday, even for something low-priority.
How Renata reflects
"Even a P4 has a target. I shouldn't have assumed low priority meant it could wait past Monday."
Case 4 · P3
A client asks a general question about recommended storage conditions for one of Altiva's enzymes — standard priority. They ask Monday at 14:00. Renata replies Tuesday at 9:00.
SLA met. P3 response time is 8 business hours, which lands the target within the next business day. A reply by 9:00 Tuesday is right on target.
How Renata reflects
"This is exactly the pace a P3 should have — no need to rush it like a P1, but no need to let it drift either."
Case 5 · Exclusion
A client's production line goes down, and they immediately cite the SLA demanding a 1-hour fix. The cause turns out to be a malfunction in the client's own equipment, unrelated to Altiva's product.
Excluded from SLA. Under 3.4, issues involving the client's own equipment rather than Altiva's product are explicitly excluded — the SLA clock never applied here in the first place.
How Renata replies
"I understand the urgency, but this issue traces back to your own equipment, not our product — it falls outside what our SLA covers. I'm still happy to help however I can."
