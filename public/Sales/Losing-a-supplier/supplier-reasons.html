When a supplier says no

Losing a supplier is never just one story.

Mariana's crisis with VanderChem was about running low on stock. But suppliers disappear for many different reasons — and each one demands a different kind of email, a different tone, and a different plan.

"It's not always a shortage. Sometimes a supplier simply decides you are no longer worth the trouble." — common feedback from procurement teams worldwide
Before the reasons

Answer these three questions in your own words first. Then compare your instinct with the six real reasons companies lose a supplier.

What's the first sign that a supplier relationship might be in trouble?
Answer prompts
  • The first sign is usually…
  • You start to notice when…
  • I'd say the warning sign is…
Example answer
"The first sign is usually a change in communication. If a supplier who always replies quickly suddenly goes quiet, or their answers become unclear, something is probably changing on their end."
What would you say if a supplier told you, out of nowhere, that they will not sell to you anymore?
Answer prompts
  • My first reaction would be…
  • I would need to ask…
  • Before panicking, I would…
Example answer
"My first reaction would be to ask why, calmly, instead of assuming the worst. There's a big difference between a supplier who is struggling and one who is simply choosing to leave."
What's the real difference between a supplier who can't deliver and one who won't?
Answer prompts
  • The real difference is…
  • If they can't, it usually means…
  • If they won't, it usually means…
Example answer
"The real difference is control. If they can't deliver, something outside their control went wrong. If they won't, they have made a business decision — and that decision is much harder to change."
Six real reasons

These are the most common reasons a supplier stops delivering, based on how procurement and supply chain teams around the world describe it. Each one comes with the exact language used to talk about it professionally.

01
The supplier goes out of business
Sudden · no warning · highest risk

This is the worst-case scenario: the supplier becomes insolvent or simply closes down, sometimes with very little notice. There is no negotiation possible — the relationship ends because the company itself no longer exists.

"We regret to inform you that, due to insolvency proceedings, we are ceasing operations with immediate effect."
Key expressions
insolvency proceedings with immediate effect cease operations
Ideal move
Act fast, not emotionally. Activate a backup supplier immediately, and tell your own clients before they hear it from someone else.
How to protect yourself
  • Qualify at least one backup supplier for every critical input, even if you never use them.
  • Watch for early warning signs: slow replies, missed small deadlines, unusual payment requests.
  • Keep a minimum safety stock for anything you can't quickly replace.
How to resolve it
  • Contact the backup supplier immediately, even before confirming the full story.
  • Ask for any partial stock or in-transit shipments that can still be delivered.
  • Inform your own clients proactively, with a realistic revised timeline.
Quick check · should / must / supposed to
The moment the insolvency notice arrives, you ______ activate your backup supplier — there's no time to wait and see.
must — necessity, not a suggestion. There is no real alternative once the notice arrives.
Example: "We must confirm the replacement order today — there's no room to delay."
Talk about it
How quickly could your company really activate a backup supplier if this happened tomorrow?
Language frames"Realistically, it would take us…" · "If we're honest, our backup plan is…"
What's the first phone call you would make in the first hour?
Language frames"The first person I'd call is…" · "Before anything else, I would…"
02
A shortage or cost spike upstream
The supplier's own supplier fails
spike = a sudden price increase (also: a bump, a hike)

Sometimes the problem isn't your supplier — it's theirs. A raw material shortage or a sudden cost increase earlier in the supply chain can force them to delay, cancel, or ask for a higher price on an order they already confirmed.

"Due to a shortage of raw materials, we are unable to honor the agreed price and must revise our pricing for this order."
Key expressions
a shortage of raw materials honor the agreed price revise our pricing
Ideal move
Ask for proof of the shortage, and negotiate a partial adjustment instead of accepting a full price increase without question.
How to protect yourself
  • Diversify raw material sources when possible, so one shortage doesn't stop everything.
  • Ask suppliers for early-warning clauses in the contract, not just penalty clauses.
  • Track global price trends for the ingredients your supply chain depends on.
How to resolve it
  • Request documented proof of the shortage before accepting any price change.
  • Negotiate a partial increase or a temporary cap instead of the full adjustment.
  • Explore whether a smaller, faster shipment can bridge the gap while prices stabilize.
Quick check · should / must / supposed to
If a supplier claims a shortage, they ______ show proof before you accept a price increase.
should — a wise, professional expectation, not a legal rule in every contract.
Example: "There's a shortage of raw cocoa upstream, so the supplier should explain the delay before we simply accept it."
Talk about it
Would you accept a price increase without seeing proof of the shortage? Why or why not?
Language frames"I wouldn't accept it because…" · "It would depend on…"
How do you usually verify whether a shortage claim is genuine?
Language frames"We usually check…" · "One way to verify it is…"
03
New tariffs or export restrictions
Geopolitical · regulatory

International trade can be affected by decisions made far outside the supply chain itself. New tariffs, export bans, or sanctions can suddenly make a shipment illegal, unprofitable, or simply impossible to send.

"This shipment is now subject to new export restrictions, and we must review our compliance obligations before proceeding."
Key expressions
export restrictions compliance obligations subject to
Ideal move
Ask for a compliance-adjusted timeline, and check whether a different shipping route or product classification avoids the restriction.
How to protect yourself
  • Monitor regulatory changes in your suppliers' countries, not just your own.
  • Classify products correctly from the start; misclassification often causes delays later.
  • Build extra lead time into contracts for anything crossing borders.
How to resolve it
  • Ask for a compliance-adjusted delivery date instead of assuming the shipment is cancelled.
  • Check alternative routes, ports, or product classifications that avoid the restriction.
  • Get legal or customs guidance before responding to the supplier in writing.
Quick check · should / must / supposed to
Under the new regulation, exporters ______ hold a valid export license before shipping this product.
must — a legal requirement, with no exception.
Example: "Every exporter must renew their license annually — it's not optional."
Talk about it
How closely does your company track regulatory changes that could affect its suppliers?
Language frames"Honestly, we…" · "Our company tends to…"
Would you consider a different shipping route, even if it costs more, to avoid a delay like this?
Language frames"I would definitely consider…" · "It would depend on how much…"
04
The supplier is at full capacity
Demand outgrows supply

When demand grows faster than production, suppliers often have to choose who gets served first. Smaller or newer clients are usually the first to lose priority — not because of a problem, but because of simple math.

"Our production capacity is fully allocated for this quarter, and we are unable to accommodate additional orders at this time."
Key expressions
fully allocated accommodate additional orders prioritize existing commitments
Ideal move
Ask for a firm date when capacity frees up, and start qualifying a second supplier in parallel — don't wait for the problem to repeat itself.
How to protect yourself
  • Build a real relationship with a second supplier before you actually need them.
  • Forecast demand early enough that your supplier can plan around it.
  • Ask for priority clauses in high-volume contracts, not just price terms.
How to resolve it
  • Ask for a firm date when capacity will open up again.
  • Offer to split the order, if a partial shipment keeps your production moving.
  • Start activating the backup supplier in parallel, without waiting for a final answer.
Quick check · should / must / supposed to
According to the original contract, this order ______ be delivered by the 15th — the "fully allocated" excuse doesn't change that.
was supposed to — the agreed date, regardless of the supplier's capacity problem.
Example: "The container was supposed to clear customs on Tuesday, but the paperwork wasn't ready."
Talk about it
How would you react if your biggest supplier suddenly prioritized a bigger client over you?
Language frames"My first reaction would be…" · "I think I would…"
At what point would you start actively looking for a second supplier — before or after this happens?
Language frames"I would start looking…" · "In my opinion, the right moment is…"
05
An unresolved dispute
Quality, payment, or contract

Sometimes a supplier stops shipping on purpose — not because they can't, but because there is an open disagreement: a quality complaint, a late payment, or a broken clause in the contract.

"We are withholding shipment until the outstanding balance is settled, pending resolution of this matter."
Key expressions
withhold shipment outstanding balance pending resolution of
Ideal move
Solve the real problem quickly and in writing. The shipment almost always starts again once the dispute is truly settled, not just discussed.
How to protect yourself
  • Resolve small disagreements quickly, before they grow into a shipment freeze.
  • Keep every quality and payment issue documented in writing from day one.
  • Set clear escalation steps in the contract for handling disputes.
How to resolve it
  • Address the real issue directly and put any agreement in writing immediately.
  • Avoid threats until every reasonable step to resolve it has been tried.
  • Confirm a resumed shipment date as soon as the dispute is settled.
Quick check · should / must / supposed to
Before threatening legal action, you ______ try one more direct conversation to resolve the dispute.
should — sensible advice before escalating to something more drastic.
Example: "You should always request things in writing before escalating a dispute."
Talk about it
Should a supplier ever be allowed to withhold shipment over a disagreement? Under what conditions?
Language frames"I think a supplier should only…" · "In my view, this is only acceptable if…"
How would you resolve a dispute like this without damaging the relationship permanently?
Language frames"The best way to resolve it would be…" · "I would try to…"
06
A strategic decision to leave
The choice was theirs, not yours

Sometimes there is no crisis at all — the supplier simply decides, for their own business reasons, to stop serving a market, a client size, or a product line. It is the hardest kind to hear, because there is often nothing to fix.

"As part of a strategic review, we have decided to discontinue this product line, which is no longer part of our core business."
Key expressions
strategic review discontinue no longer part of
Ideal move
Accept the decision calmly, ask for a clear transition timeline, and begin your search for a new supplier right away.
How to protect yourself
  • Never depend on a single supplier for a product line critical to your business.
  • Review contracts regularly, so notice periods and exit clauses are always current.
  • Stay alert to shifts in the supplier's business strategy, not just their delivery record.
How to resolve it
  • Accept the decision without trying to negotiate something that isn't about price.
  • Get a clear transition timeline and any remaining volume in writing.
  • Start actively searching for a replacement the same week you hear the news.
Quick check · should / must / supposed to
Under standard notice periods, a supplier ______ give at least 90 days' warning before ending a long-term contract.
is supposed to — the expected practice under typical contract terms.
Example: "A landlord is supposed to give 30 days' notice before ending a lease."
Talk about it
Is 90 days' notice enough time to find a replacement for a critical supplier?
Language frames"In my opinion, 90 days is…" · "That would depend on…"
How do you think a company should react when there is nothing left to negotiate?
Language frames"I believe a company should…" · "The most professional reaction would be…"
Now you resolve it

Read each situation. Choose the response that matches the real reason behind it — not just any polite reply.

Situation A: Your main supplier emails to say they are fully booked this quarter and cannot take your next three orders.
Situation B: A supplier you have worked with for years tells you, calmly, that they are discontinuing the product line you buy, as part of a wider strategic review.
Resolving it

Whatever the reason behind the problem, resolving it well always uses the same five moves. This language works across all six situations above — it is not tied to any single reason.

1. Asking for clarity
Before reacting, find out exactly what happened
Could you confirm a specific date for…?
Do you have an estimated timeline for…?
Could you clarify what caused this change?
2. Proposing alternatives
Offer a real solution, not just a complaint
Would it be possible to split the shipment?
As an alternative, could we consider…?
We would like to propose a temporary solution.
3. Setting a deadline
Be firm on time without being aggressive
We would need this confirmed by [date].
Unless we hear from you by…, we will have to…
We must have an answer before we can proceed.
4. Protecting the relationship
Stay diplomatic, even under pressure
We understand this is a difficult situation for you too.
We value the partnership we have built over the years.
We hope we can find a solution that works for both sides.
5. Escalating carefully
Show what could happen next, without ending the relationship
This puts us in a difficult position with our own clients.
We may need to explore other options if this continues.
We would have to reconsider the terms of our agreement.
Sample email · supplier at full capacity

Dear Mr. Alves,

Thank you for letting us know about the current situation. Could you confirm a specific date when your capacity might open up again for smaller accounts like ours?

In the meantime, would it be possible to split the shipment, even partially, so our production line does not stop completely?

We would need this confirmed by Friday, since we have our own commitments to manage on this end.

We understand this is a difficult situation for you too, and we value the partnership we have built over the years. That said, we may need to explore other options if this continues beyond this quarter.

We hope to hear from you soon.

Best regards,
Mariana

Talking about obligation

Every reason above eventually leads to the same three questions: what was supposed to happen, what must happen now, and what someone should have done differently. These three modals carry very different weight — mixing them up can make you sound either too soft or unnecessarily aggressive.

Should / Shouldn't
Advice — what is wise, not obligatory
Form
should / shouldn't + base verb
In context
"You should ask for a firm date in writing before you tell the client anything."
"You shouldn't accept an excuse without proof."
No "to" after should — NOT "you should to ask".
Must / Mustn't
Strong obligation — a rule, or something necessary now
Form
must / mustn't + base verb
In context
"We must confirm a backup supplier before the end of this week."
"A distributor mustn't resell the enzyme without written approval."
Mustn't = forbidden. It is not the negative of "have to" — that's "don't have to" (not necessary).
(Be) Supposed To
An agreed expectation — often one that was broken
Form
was/were/is/are + supposed to + base verb
In context
"The shipment was supposed to leave Rotterdam on Monday."
"They're not supposed to change the price without notice."
Don't drop the "to" — NOT "supposed leave", but "supposed to leave".

Now see all three working together in two real conversations Mariana has after a supplier problem.

Call 1 · Mariana and Lars (VanderChem)
After a missed shipment · reasons 2 & 4 combined
Mariana: Lars, the shipment was supposed to leave Rotterdam on Monday. It's Thursday and I still don't have a tracking number.
Lars: I know, Mariana, and I'm sorry. We must review our production schedule — there was a shortage of raw materials we didn't expect.
Mariana: I understand things happen, but you should have told us before the ship date, not after.
Lars: You're right. We should have called as soon as we knew. That was our mistake.
Mariana: Going forward, what must change on your side so this doesn't happen again?
Lars: We must build in a 48-hour buffer before every confirmed ship date. And honestly, we shouldn't confirm a date until raw materials are physically in the warehouse.
Mariana: That sounds reasonable. For now, though, I must have a new date in writing by end of day.
Lars: Understood. You'll have it by 5pm your time.
Call 2 · Mariana and Rafael Moura (Frigorex)
Explaining the delay to the client
Mariana: Rafael, I need to tell you something directly. The enzyme shipment was supposed to arrive today, and it won't.
Rafael: That's a serious problem for us, Mariana. Our production line runs on that schedule.
Mariana: I know, and I take responsibility. You shouldn't have to find out from anyone but me first.
Rafael: I appreciate that. What must happen now for us to keep production running?
Mariana: We must ship you an emergency partial batch tomorrow morning, and I'll personally confirm it before you start your shift.
Rafael: And the rest of the order?
Mariana: You should have the remaining volume by Friday. I'm not going to promise a date I'm not sure of again.
Rafael: I respect that. Going forward, we're supposed to receive a 48-hour warning for anything like this — can we put that in writing?
Mariana: Absolutely. That should be standard practice, not something we negotiate after a crisis.

Documents & evidence, by reason

Real disputes are rarely settled by an email alone. These are the documents that actually get requested and exchanged for each of the six reasons. Hover any name for the Portuguese translation.

01 · Supplier goes out of business
Insolvency filing (public notice)
registro público de insolvência
The official public record showing a company has formally declared insolvency.
Notice of cessation of business
aviso de cessação de atividades
A formal written notice from the supplier stating that operations have stopped.
02 · Shortage or cost spike
Force Majeure Certificate
certificado de força maior
An official document, often issued by a Chamber of Commerce, confirming that an unexpected event really stopped a company from following its contract. Real and widely used in international trade.
Supplier's Notice of Force Majeure
aviso de força maior do fornecedor
The supplier's own written notice using this clause, describing the event and its effect.
03 · Tariffs or export restrictions
Export license
licença de exportação
Official government authorization required to legally ship certain goods abroad.
Certificate of Origin
certificado de origem
A document certifying which country the goods were manufactured in, often required to apply tariffs correctly.
04 · Supplier at full capacity
Order confirmation / backlog report
confirmação de pedido / relatório de pedidos pendentes
A document showing the supplier's current order queue, sometimes shared to show real limits on capacity.
Capacity utilization report
relatório de utilização de capacidade
A report showing how much of a factory's production capacity is currently in use.
05 · Unresolved dispute
Certificate of Analysis (CoA)
certificado de análise
A lab report confirming a product's quality specifications, often at the center of quality disputes.
Notice of Breach
notificação de quebra de contrato
A formal letter stating that one party has failed to meet its contractual obligations.
06 · Strategic decision to leave
Termination notice
aviso de rescisão
The formal written notice ending a contract, stating the effective date and the stated reason.
Notice period letter
carta com o prazo de aviso prévio
States how many days or months remain before the contract formally ends, as agreed in the original terms.
